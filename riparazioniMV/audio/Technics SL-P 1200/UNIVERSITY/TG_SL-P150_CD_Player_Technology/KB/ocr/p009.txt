8-1 Lead-in and Lead-out Signals

The lead-in signal is located just in
front of where the signal area starts,
while the lead-out signals appears right
after the end of the signal area. These
two signals are used to control the
movement of the optical pick-up.

8-2 Table of Contents (TOC)

Written in the lead-in area is the time
information on control codes including
the start time of each selection as well
as the total number and playing time of
selections, which can be read out before
performance starts for the convenience
of program search and so on. Such
information can also be displayed.

8-3 Control Codes

These codes distinguish between 2-
channel recording and 4—channel
recording. They can also detect whether

preemphasis is given to a particular
recording. Furthermore, they can even
change the circuit connections of the
player automatically according to the
detected conditions of the disc.

| 8-4 Music Start Flag

The music start flag is provided in the
blank space between selections. By
counting this flag, therefore it is
possible to locate any desired selection
on the disc. Even when the end of a
selection fades in to the beginning of
the next, the flag can be inserted a
minimum of two seconds in front of the
second selection so that such selections
can be recognized not as a single piece
of music but as two separate selections.

8-5 Track Number and Index

Selections recorded on the disc can be
numbered from 1 to 99. Each bar in a
piece of music can also be addressed up
to 99.

With the time code function, you can
tell the time lapse from the beginning
of each slection in minutes, seconds,
and 1/75 of a second. During the blank

Space between selections, the time is
counted down.

As exemplified above, the control bits
can be used in a variety of ways. They
not only let you locate any portion of
music exactly and quickly but also allow
random playback in any order you set.
And despite the long playing time of an
hour on one side, access to the desired
point can be achieved almost instantly.
This is one of the greatest advantages
of the CD system which cannot be found

in any other system.

9. Analog Signal Versus Digital Signal

An analog signal is a continuous signal
in two respects: value and time.

A digital signal is discrete, both in
value and in time. Discrete in time
means that the signal is defined on
discrete intervals of time. Discrete in
value means that the signal has finite
number of possible values.

A digital signal is a sequence of words,
each representing the value of a sample.
The value of a word or digital signal,
the binary code, is a combination of "1"
(on) and "0" (off). The composing parts
of the binary code are the bits.

Bit is an abbreviation for "binary
digit". It is the smallest unit that can
have a meaning in binary notation, and
that can only have two values, "ON" or
"OFF", represented as "1" or "0".

The higher the number of bits used in
the binary code, the higher the
resolution and the combination of bits,
which means the higher the accuracy to
describe the value of the sample.
