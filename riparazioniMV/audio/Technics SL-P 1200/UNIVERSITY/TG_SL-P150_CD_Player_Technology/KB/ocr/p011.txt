Although the sampling operation may seem
to introduce a rather drastic
modification of the input signal:

-Since it ignores all the signal changes
that occur between the sampling times.
-It can be shown that the sampling
process itself, in principle, removes
no information whatsoever, as long as
the sampling frequency is at least
twice the highest frequency that is

present in the input signal.

This is the famous Nyquist criterion on
sampling (also called shannon criterion
or shannon theorem).

Since the highest frequency found in an
audio signal is approximately 20KHz, and
as selecting an unnecessary high
sampling frequency would increase both
complexity and cost of the conversion
circuitry, a frequency of 44.1KHz has
been chosen as the standard for the
compact disc.

The correctness of the Nyquist cirterion
can be understood when we consider the
spectra of the input and output signals,
see figure 10-3.

If we consider an input signal i(t)
Which has a maximum frequency f max, its
spectrum may have any from between 0 Hz
and f max (1); the sampling signal's(t),
having a fixed frequency fs, can be
represented by one signal line at fs
(2). The sampling process is equivalent
to multiplication of i(t) ands (t), and

the spectrum of the output signal, as
shown in figure 10-2, can be seen to
contain the same spectrum as the input
signal, together with the repetition of
the same spectrum, modulated around
multiples of the sampling frequency.

AS a consequence, proper low-pass
filtering can completely isolate and
thus completely recover the input
signal.

From figure 10-3 it can also be
understood easily that f must be greater
than 2 x f max. If this would not be the
case, the original spectrum would
overlap with the modulated part of the
spectrum, and consequently be
inseparable from is, see figure 10-4.

For example, a 20KHz signal sampled at
35 KHz would produce a 5 KHz difference
frequency. This phenomenon is known as
aliasing and must be avoided by all
means.

For this reason, a very sharp cut-off
filter (anti-aliasing filter) is
absolutely required in the signal path
to remove all the unwanted harmonics out
of the input signal before the sampling
is performed. If this is not done,
aliasing will cause unremovable

distortion components.
